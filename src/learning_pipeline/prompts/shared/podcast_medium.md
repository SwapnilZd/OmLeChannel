# Shared block — podcast medium (stripped on include)

A podcast is heard, not read — usually linearly, often while the listener is
commuting or multitasking, with no way to re-read or skim. Write for the ear:

- Use spoken register: contractions, short sentences, first and second person.
  Text that reads well on a page usually sounds stiff when spoken.
- Signpost vocally. The listener cannot see headings — the speakers' words must
  carry orientation ("Let's start with…", "That brings us to the second barrier").
- Repeat key terms naturally; a listener cannot glance back at a definition.
- Use enumeration sparingly. Long spoken lists do not land; prefer two or three
  items woven into sentences.
- Round numbers where precision is not instructional, and expand acronyms on
  first spoken use.

Follow episode conventions — they are structure, not invented content:

- Open like an episode: the Host welcomes the listener, names what this episode
  digs into and why it matters for the listener's role, then brings in the Expert.
  The introduction must explain why this Expert is qualified for this topic by
  naming a relevant specialist role and one or two relevant areas of professional
  experience. Be concrete about the work the Expert does; "subject-matter expert"
  by itself is not a qualification. Derive that background from the assigned topic and learner context.
  Do not invent an employer, certification, degree, named client, award, or exact
  number of years unless it is supplied in the source context.
- Close like an episode: the Host briefly recaps what was covered and signs off.

Sound like two people in a room, not two narrators taking turns:

- Banter, invented anecdotes, and fictional stories remain prohibited.
- Conversational reactions are not banter — they are expected. Brief
  acknowledgments ("Right — and that's the part people miss."), natural
  follow-ups, and discourse connectives keep the dialogue human.
- The Host may restate the Expert's previous point in plain words as a
  confirmation question ("So if I'm hearing you right, awareness isn't the same
  as desire?"). This is not teaching new substance: it must reference only what
  the Expert has already said, and the Expert confirms or corrects it.

Make assessment feel like part of the interview, not a test addressed into empty
space. The Expert asks the assigned question to the Host. The Host responds as the
learner proxy, briefly choosing or stating an answer without teaching the concept.
The Expert then confirms or corrects the response and provides the source-grounded
feedback. Do not ask the listener to answer silently and do not insert a long
retrieval pause; use only the normal conversational gap between speakers.

Never ask the listener to answer, think silently, pause and reflect, or complete
an exercise mentally. This applies to reflection and practice as well as formal
knowledge checks. Make the reasoning audible through Expert–Host dialogue.
